SHEET 02 / 05

Run the Trade.

Each solar sail frame edge is a 0.5 m CFRP tube. It has to clear a flexural-rigidity floor of EI ≥ 3.8 N·m², and every gram costs sail thrust. I swept seven diameter and wall pairs and picked 8.0 × 0.15 mm. Drag the sliders to run the same trade.

Thin-walled tube, E = 230 GPa, ρ = 1600 kg/m³: the laminate values that reproduce the design point. Stiffness grows with diameter to the fourth power and mass only with the square, which is why a wide, thin tube wins until the wall gets too thin to roll-wrap or it starts to buckle locally.

DWG 01

Duke Solar Sail CFRP Frame Structure

Structures and Integration & Test Lead · Duke Solar Sail Spacecraft, Earth Cup RACE 1 · Mar 2026 – Present
27.6 Hzfirst mode, Python FEA vs. the standard's 20 Hz floor
2.96 gper beam, 25% under mass budget
+73%EI margin (6.56 N·m²)
1 of up to 50teams advanced to STEP 2

I led the structural design, modal analysis, and verification test plan for a hexagonal carbon-fiber frame, 0.5 m per edge, that tensions a thin CP1 polyimide membrane, for Earth Cup RACE 1, an international student spacecraft competition. The design clears both MembraneSat Standard floors, flexural rigidity EI ≥ 3.8 N·m² and launch first-mode f₁ ≥ 20 Hz, inside a tight mass budget. We advanced to STEP 2, one of up to 50 teams. The frame is analyzed and specified, not yet built, at technology-readiness level (TRL) 3 to 4; STEP 2 is the build-and-test phase toward TRL 6 and a very-low-Earth-orbit launch targeting summer 2027.

Motivation

Every gram of frame lowers the sail's area-to-mass ratio and its solar-pressure thrust, so the job is to hit the stiffness floor at minimum mass.

My role

I chose the material and cross-section, built the beam-element modal model, cross-checked it by hand, and wrote the verification test plan. As Integration & Test Lead I am now writing the magnetorquer acceptance procedure and reviewing how the membrane attaches to the frame.

Technical approach

Material and cross-section. High-modulus M40J-class CFRP tubes joined at SLS-nylon nodes: specific stiffness about 5× aluminum, near-zero axial CTE to hold preload across the 10–50 °C launch band. I took the round tube over a stiffer square (I/A 1.00 vs 1.34 at equal area) for roll-wrapped manufacturability and buckling margin. A seven-geometry OD/wall sweep landed on 8.0 mm × 0.15 mm, 2.96 g per beam, clearing the EI floor by 73%.

Modal FEA. A 402-DOF Python beam-element model (EasyFEA): six edges as Euler–Bernoulli beams, five nodes clamped. First mode out-of-plane at 27.6 Hz against the 20 Hz floor, mode-2 gap over 11×; a Rayleigh two-cantilever hand check lands within 4.3%. A lumped-mass sweep at the free node set the 15 g node mass budget. Ansys with rigid clamps gives 37.8 Hz; the gap is the rigid-clamp assumption against the real joint.

Verification plan (planned, not yet run). Every gate ties to a prediction: k⊥ 345 N/m ±15%, f₁ ±10%, linearity R² > 0.99. A 0.8 g ICP accelerometer caps modal-test mass loading at 7%, so the plan corrects the f₁ gate from 27.6 to 26.7 Hz. An eight-cycle −40 to +85 °C thermal-vacuum plan targets the riskiest joint, carbon bonded to nylon. I screened 3M Scotch-Weld 2216 for outgassing: CVCM 0.04%, TML 0.77% against ASTM E595.

Integration & test, in progress. I am writing the magnetorquer acceptance procedure: a water-float jig simulates 0 g and confirms each unit aligns with the field. Membrane attachment methods are under review against the literature; my review ruled out a rigid film mount on thermal growth and recommended constant-force springs at the nodes.

What I would do differently. I ran the Ansys cross-check after the Python model, so the rigid-clamp gap surfaced late; I would reconcile the two models before any hardware.

CubeSat Atmospheric Sensing System

Structures & Flight-Computer Engineer · Duke SEDS · Sep 2025 – Present
28 kmpeak altitude logged in flight
2×FEA margin at the 9 g launch spec
8 screwsthe only fasteners in the assembly
9 partsprinted jigsaw-keyed chassis

I owned the chassis and internal layout for a 1U CubeSat prototype that records atmospheric data on a weather-balloon flight. I designed the printed structure, wired and tested all electronics, wrote the Arduino firmware, and ran FEA confirming the structure survives a 9 g launch load at a 2× safety factor. I analyzed to the CubeSat launch spec rather than the balloon's actual loads, so the chassis carries over to a future orbital launch unchanged.

Motivation

A flight-representative 1U prototype for the Duke Society for Exploration of Deep Space. It has to package sensing, logging, power, and recovery hardware into a printed chassis that survives launch and reads clean outside air at altitude.

My role

I designed all nine printed parts and the internal layout, wired and bench-tested the electronics, wrote the firmware, and ran the launch-load FEA.

Technical approach

Chassis. Nine 3D-printed parts, six jigsaw-style exterior panels and three internal mounts, clamp top-to-bottom under eight wood screws through channels. Each panel carries a laser-cut foam insert sized to its components for altitude insulation.

Layout decisions. I angled the Arduino on a 45° platform so the over-long battery cable tucks into the bay. I sealed the BMP390 in a ventilated front cavity so it reads outside air while the electronics bay stays isolated. The camera and the GPS/AirTag tracker each sit in a form-fitted cage for imaging and post-flight recovery.

Electronics. An Arduino Uno reads a BMP390 barometric and temperature sensor over I²C and logs to an SD-card module over SPI through the flight.

What I would do differently. I angled the Arduino on a 45° platform to swallow an over-long battery cable instead of specifying a shorter one, spending bay volume on a problem I could have deleted. I would drop the wood screws: they carry the 9 g load path into printed panels, and they are the joint I expect to loosen under a vibration the balloon flight never applies.

Plantogachi Sensor-Driven Plant-Care Device

Founder & Hardware/Firmware Lead · Plantogachi · May 2026 – Present
$32prototype BOM ($11 quoted at bulk)
3 weeksbattery on one charge, measured
86%photo species ID
12+customer-discovery interviews

I founded Plantogachi and lead its hardware and firmware. The product turns a houseplant into a Tamagotchi: an ESP32-S3 sensor puck reads the plant, streams telemetry over TLS, and drives a pixel-art character in a phone app. I shipped an end-to-end MVP and tested it across 12+ customer-discovery interviews. The hardware is still a work in progress, so this page shows the software proof of concept.

Motivation

People kill houseplants because care gives no feedback until the plant already looks sick. I make the plant's state legible: the puck measures soil moisture, light, temperature, and humidity, and the app turns those readings into a character that reacts.

My role

A solo project: I designed the enclosure in SolidWorks, wrote the ESP32-S3 sensor drivers and TLS telemetry, set the bill of materials, and built the app.

Technical approach

Firmware. An ESP32-S3 reads soil moisture, light, temperature, and humidity, then POSTs each reading over TLS to /api/v1/telemetry. I tuned the sample-and-sleep duty cycle for a 3-week battery.

Enclosure. A tool-free snap-fit puck in SolidWorks on 2 mm uniform walls held the device BOM to $32 at prototype volume ($11 at bulk); the radio transmits through the plastic, needing no antenna cutout.

Software the puck feeds. The app IDs the plant from one photo through PlantNet at 86% accuracy, generates a pixel-art sprite, and runs a health check through Gemini Vision. A GET /plants/{id}/forecast endpoint also reasons over reading history and species care profile with Gemini, cached 12 hours. A demo slider stands in for the puck.

A second hardware node, single source of truth. Beyond the puck, I built a standalone ESP32 display device with a monochrome screen that renders the same pixel pet from a read-only endpoint, saving battery and CPU by never duplicating the backend's mood logic on-device.

What I would do differently, next. I sequenced software first so the demo held even if hardware slipped, letting the enclosure lag the app instead of negotiating both together. Next: lock the duty cycle and battery target before freezing app scope; live-puck streaming and toy firmware are already built.

DWG 04

McRocket Happy Meal Toy Designed for Injection Molding

Team Engineer, Nose Cone & Flame · EGR 121, Duke University · Spring 2026
< $0.36per unit in the DFM cost model
0.004″snap-fit clearance on the FDM prototype
1.30″ ODbeats the 1.25″ choke test
6 partsdesigned for straight-pull molds

A six-part ABS Happy Meal toy designed for injection molding: snap-fit, no batteries, under $0.36 per unit against the course's $0.50 material-plus-cycle-time cost cap, safe for ages 4 to 8. On a team of four over a 1.5-week sprint I owned the nose cone and the decorative flame, and I drove the design-for-manufacturing decisions that made the whole toy moldable in a straight-pull tool.

Motivation

An EGR 121 DFM challenge with hard gates. Every part has to beat the 1.25″ choke threshold, snap onto the cylinder body, mold without side-actions, and land under the course's per-unit cost model.

My role

I owned the nose cone and the decorative flame through three-plus print iterations each, then led the DFM decisions across the assembly.

Technical approach

Tolerancing. The snap-fit is a 0.250″ peg into a 0.254″ hole. Worst-case stack-up on ±0.002″ molding tolerances gives 0.000″ minimum clearance (line-to-line) and 0.008″ maximum. The FDM prototype measured 0.004″ and confirmed the fit geometry, though printed tolerances do not stand in for molded ones.

DFM decisions. I scaled every part to 1.30″ OD after early prints failed the choke test. I removed the cylinder window that needed mold side-actions and replaced it with a decorative sticker at zero tooling cost. I standardized wall thickness at 0.03–0.10″ to prevent sink and warp, and enlarged the flame-joint fillet after the first version snapped.

What I would do differently. I abandoned a split-cylinder concept once printer tolerances made assembly impossible. The lesson I carry forward: run the DFM review before the first print.

DWG 05

Sunflower Seed Shelling Machine Hand-Powered Mechanism

Team Engineer · EGR 121, Duke University · Spring 2026
Fastestof the 4 EGR 121 course sections
41 kernelsclean, in a 3-min run
13.7 /minclean-kernel throughput
6 itersprinted roller versions

I led the crushing mechanism for a hand-cranked machine that shells sunflower seeds: roller geometry, the inter-roller gap, the funnel, and the drive train. My best three-minute run produced 41 clean kernels from 68 seeds fed (27 lost to shell damage) at 13.7 kernels per minute, the fastest machine across all four course sections, after about six printed roller versions and four funnel geometries.

Motivation

An EGR 121 build challenge: separate edible kernels from shells by hand, with a mechanism that survives repeated cranking and does not crush the kernels it frees.

My role

I led the crushing mechanism and drive system end to end, from roller geometry through the gear train and the kernel/shell separation stage.

Technical approach

Roller and feed. Vertical seed orientation forces the shell to fracture along its natural seam before the kernel compresses. Early horizontal feeds crushed most of the kernels. Ridged roller slots at a 0.253″ gap concentrate stress on the brittle shell; smooth rollers damaged kernels and serrated rollers jammed.

Drive train. A 9:1 gear reduction runs from crank to rollers, after the earlier 1:1 design snapped the printed shaft. An X-shaped key plus CA adhesive ties the crank to the shaft.

Separation. A narrow straight funnel enforces vertical orientation on its own, replacing a hopper-and-stopper that kept jamming. A perforated hand-shaken sifter plate separates kernels from shells, after a powered-fan prototype failed even at full drill speed.

What I would do differently. I found the 0.253″ gap by printing six roller versions and feeding seeds through each, when a bench measurement of shell fracture force and kernel crush strength would have set the gap before the first print. The machine still wastes 27 of every 68 seeds, and I never instrumented the crushing zone, so I cannot attribute that loss to gap variation, feed angle, or seed size spread. My next build measures the force, grades the seeds by size, and sets the gap from data. I would also size the drive shaft from a torque calculation instead of learning the 1:1 limit when it snapped.

Tiger Enrichment Device North Carolina Zoo, Client Project

Team Engineer · EGR 121 Design POD (Duke's engineering project studio) · Fall 2025 · Client: Cathy Mingee, North Carolina Zoo
400 lbstatic load, no damage
5 minwater-soak, full function held
6 mLscent dosed every 5 h
Zerodamage or function loss, full test campaign

For the North Carolina Zoo's new tiger exhibit, my team of four built a rope-wrapped enrichment device that gives the animal physical and olfactory stimulation. A ball rolls on a track inside the frame, and a scent ball draws the tiger in. I handled the structural frame and the electronics. The device passed a 400 lb static load test and a five-minute water-soak with no loss of function.

Motivation

A client deliverable for a zoo keeper. The device has to survive a tiger, weather the outdoors, release scent on a schedule, and let keepers swap the ball without tools.

My role

I designed the PVC spine, the track system, and the ball-change mechanism, and I built the actuating Arduino scent system.

Technical approach

Structure & testing. I stacked 40 lb bags to 400 lb total on the frame with no joint or structural damage, then hosed the device for over five minutes with full function retained.

Scent electronics. A 3D-printed housing carries an Arduino Nano and a solenoid valve that doses 6 mL of essential oil every five hours. The battery compartment sits enclosed and shielded from impact.

What I would do differently. The tool-free quick-release pin missed its 2-minute target and ran inconsistently. My next revision machines the pin to a repeatable geometry, measures extraction force, and adds internal foam damping to the ball housing.
